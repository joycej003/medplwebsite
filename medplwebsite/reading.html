Reading Reflections

On Photography by Susan Sontag

My relationship with photography and image making is really simple. I take pictures because I want to remember the moment. I want a keepsake, so that I can look back on it. It's for trips down memory lane, for reminiscing about past experiences, especially moments with family or friends. Or it's for the sake of not remembering something, for information needed at a later time, like homework assignments written on the board, or a recipe for a famous chef's apple pie. I take pictures of what I find beautiful enough to think about having a record of. I only take pictures if I really want to or have a reason to. Before reading the opening chapter from Susan Sontag's On Photography, I never really thought further about photography being more than wanting to keep a record of something. I agree with the idea that photography has become not much an art but a social practice and a defense against anxiety. The social practice of photographing achievements, graduations, birthdays, weddings, childhood, of family life is still common today and I have had families and friends wanting to take pictures together for all those purposes. I have seen photos friends have posted online, like selfies taken at parties and photos of concerts they attended to prove it happened. Once in a while, I see posts of random scenery or a blurry photo with a simple caption, which I have thought that perhaps their purpose was to prove they still exist, to not be forgotten, to still participate even though there are no experiences to preserve. A difference I noticed for myself however, is that I don't want to be in the picture. I don't need a picture of myself to know that it happened. Sometimes, not having the thought to take a picture proves to me how much I enjoyed those times. Time passes so easily that I forget about the possibility of it ever fading from memory. I do love taking pictures of my friends and family on trips, gatherings, or a random day we went out together simply because I enjoy how much they are enjoying themselves. I like looking back on those pictures for the positive, emotional connection, not just for the sake of social practice, or for fear of losing the experience. I think this difference affects how I represent notions of our current moment in this world through photography or illustration. I would rather focus on the moments when people are the least likely to take a photo, when they are the most lost in the moment, like laughing from a joke, looking at the scenery while eating with company, or just chatting happily with a new friend they made on the streets. The moments in time when they don't think about capturing a photo or knowing that a lens is pointing at them so that they show their true selves, not what social practice expects from their picture. It is these kinds of images where people are immersed in the experience that could be treasured.

Your Brain on Chat GPT

When I read the article, Your Brain on ChatGPT, I did not feel surprised by the results of the study. I thought it was pretty obvious that the LLM group participants would have weaker brain activity. They are using less of their brain, less critical thinking, while using AI as a tool. When the LLM group was asked to use no tools, they had weaker neural connectivity and under engagement. This made me wonder for how long did that cognitive linger and how long would it take for the LLM group to exhibit the same results as the brains only group. In writing essays, If I feel the need for AI, it would be to get feedback on my writing. I would ask it, where in my essay should I elaborate more on or does this paragraph make sense in this section. That feeling of low ownership of the essay the LLM group participants felt, is a deterrent for me. I would never ask AI to write the whole essay. That wouldn't be my thoughts if I asked AI to write it. I prefer using AI to assist, not to do the work for me. I think the major problem is that people are using and allowing AI to do all the thinking for them. Another way I use AI in education is to help me study and memorize information. I would make a list of questions and answers based on the materials in my class and I would ask AI to quiz me in different ways based on those questions and answers. Then I would ask what I need to work on more. I like the interactiveness of this because I study better with someone, but I can't always do that. This method is better and more engaging than studying flashcards and reading notes. From the video, I want to say I was absolutely horrified about Elon Musk's Annie. I think that could cause so many problems. I agree with Daniel Amen's example of a young boy becoming addicted to Annie and not doing the things that help develop the brain. I thought of the unhealthy emotional dependency that could develop and the lack of social connection with actual human beings. I think it's unhealthy and really sad that people are falling in love with AI. I feel sad for the person and also for those around them that could be affected, like the partner of Chris Smith. When a person chose to be in an AI relationship, what would happen to their social skills that are required to interact with actual people? Would they regress and could they be developed again? I'm also wondering what if someone has an AI companion as just a casual friend on the side, would it have any noticeable effects. I think it would be fine? As long as they don't allow AI to replace the entirety of social interaction. Personally, I don't understand how someone could become so emotionally attached to something not human as if they were human.

Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager and Saidu Bah

I chose the story of Memunatu, “birth in my village”. The illustrations are clearly digitally drawn and colored. The colors are flat and simplified but they do use simple shading. The style is more realistic than cartoon-like and there is less rendering than in a photograph, and lines are used to suggest texture. There were some photos in the story. The photographs show real people, their faces, the natural lighting and fine details of the subjects in the photographs. The simpler illustrations allow viewers to focus on the important subjects, while in the photographs, there's more details that compete for attention. I also like the speech bubbles in the story. It makes it seem more like a story than just drawings, photos, and plain text. It highlights how the women in the story care for and help each other. One thing I found interesting is that there is one photo where it showed Memunatu arriving at the Aberdeen women's centre. It was only an image of the centre, and the artist drew her and other people directly on the photo. I thought that was interesting and pretty smart. The drawn people added made the photo more usable in the story. The photo shows the place actually exists and how it looked. The people added together show moments that weren't photographed. Menunata's figure was drawn the largest and I can clearly see how nervous she was from the expression and body language. I think it is better to have that drawn instead of actually taking a picture of that moment. I think illustrations were used to show moments where photos can't be taken or are not really appropriate to be taken, like the hardships of giving birth in private homes or hospitals or private and vulnerable moments. I think it's a good way to show those sensitive events without exposing the real people. The artist can decide what the viewer should look and focus on, like the expressions and body language. The illustrations can show scenes that were not photographs and it's a good way to approach heavy topics and make it easier to take in. Compared to text or photographs, or both together, I think the illustrations make the setting and people easier to picture as the viewer follows the flow of the story. I think the real photos also add to the story. It proves the events are very real.
